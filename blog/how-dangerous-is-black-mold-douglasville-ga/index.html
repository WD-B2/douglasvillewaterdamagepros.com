Is Black Mold Dangerous? What Douglasville Owners Should Do

Color does not identify a mold. This guide covers what you can learn from what you see and which steps to take, leaving health questions to a doctor.

Written by the Douglasville Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 14, 2026.

Black mold is a popular name, not a scientific one. Many molds look dark, and color alone does not tell you what species is growing. What you can see is a sign of moisture, and moisture is the thing to fix.

What the Color Tells You

A dark spot on drywall or caulk can be several different molds or even dirt. You cannot name the species by sight, and the same species can vary in color as it ages. The practical conclusion is that any visible growth on a building material should be treated the same way.

That means finding the moisture, removing materials that cannot be cleaned and drying the space. Questions about how mold affects a particular person belong with a doctor, and this page does not offer medical advice.

Steps to Take When You Find It

  1. Find the moisture source and stop it.
  2. Keep the area closed off. Do not sweep, sand or run a fan across the growth.
  3. Photograph the area and note the date.
  4. Measure the area. Small patches on hard surfaces can be cleaned, but larger areas need containment.
  5. Call a remediation crew if the growth is on drywall, inside a wall or in a large area.

Testing: When It Helps

Air or surface tests can document that mold is present and sometimes identify types. They do not replace fixing the moisture. Tests are most useful when you need an independent record for an insurance claim, a lease dispute or a sale.

If you hire a tester, choose one who is separate from the remediation crew. That separation gives you a second set of eyes.

Rebuilding After Removal

Once the materials are removed and the area is dry, rebuilding can begin. New drywall, insulation and trim should go in only after moisture readings are normal. Prime and paint after the patch is dry.

Questions to Ask a Remediation Company

  • What is the plan for containment, and how will you protect the rest of the house?
  • How will you find and document the moisture source?
  • Which materials will be removed, and which will be cleaned?
  • Do you recommend independent testing, and who would do it?
  • What does the final moisture reading need to be before rebuilding?

A clear answer to each question shows that the company has a process. Vague answers or pressure to sign the same day are reasons to slow down and compare other options.

What Insurance May Do

Many policies treat mold as a result of a covered water event or limit mold coverage to a set amount. Report the water loss early and keep the timeline documented. If the mold appeared after a long period with no known leak, expect more questions from the adjuster about the cause.

Cost and Scope in General Terms

Remediation scope depends on the size of the affected area, the materials involved, the location in the house and whether the moisture source is fixed. Attics and crawl spaces add access challenges, and large areas require containment and negative air.

Ask for a written scope that lists the area, the materials to be removed and the steps for drying and cleaning. A written scope lets you compare proposals on the same terms.

What to Tell the Property Owner or Landlord

Renters should report visible growth to the landlord in writing and keep a copy. Include photos, the date you noticed it and any recent leak. Written notice protects you and starts the repair clock.

Owners selling a home should document the repair and the readings. Buyers often ask for that record, and it helps the sale move forward.

Finally, keep the focus on what you can control. You cannot identify a mold by color, but you can find the moisture, remove wet materials and dry the space. Those steps reduce the conditions that growth needs, whatever the species.

What to Do When You Find Growth

  • Do not disturb the area or run a fan across it.
  • Photograph it and note the date.
  • Find and stop the moisture source.
  • Ask a doctor about any health concerns.

EPA guidance on mold in buildings stresses finding and fixing moisture, and ANSI/IICRC S520 is the published standard for mold remediation. Neither tells you a species from its color.

Mold Questions in a Georgia Home

Georgia humidity keeps indoor air damp for much of the year. Running air conditioning and a dehumidifier in the affected area helps, and so does fixing the drainage around the foundation and the condensate line on the air handler.

Ask whether your state licenses mold work before hiring. Rules differ across states and change over time, so confirm current requirements with the agency that oversees contractors in your state.
