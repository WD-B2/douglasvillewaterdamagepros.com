Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Water Damage in Georgia?

Coverage depends on where the water came from. Georgia insurance guidance separates sudden leaks, appliance overflows, roof damage and flood, and each is treated differently.

Written by the Douglasville Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 14, 2026.

Most people learn how their policy treats water only after a loss. The source of the water matters more than the amount, so the first question your carrier asks is how it got in. This guide uses statements from the Georgia Office of Commissioner of Insurance and general industry practice, and your own policy language always controls.

Sudden Leaks Versus Gradual Seepage

Standard homeowners policies are usually written to respond to sudden and accidental events. A pipe that bursts overnight is the typical example. A slow drip under a sink that runs for months is often treated as a maintenance issue.

That distinction is why a record of when you found the leak matters. Note the date, take photos and save the failed part. If the leak was hidden, a moisture reading from a restoration crew can show the extent of the damage.

What Georgia Guidance Says

The Georgia Office of Commissioner of Insurance states that a standard homeowners policy does not cover flood. It also notes that water seeping in from outside may not be covered. Overflowing washing machines and dishwashers, by contrast, are normally covered.

For a damaged roof, the guidance is to cover it with a tarp and document the damage with photos. That step limits further loss, and it shows the insurer you acted to protect the property.

Flood Is a Separate Policy

Flood coverage is usually bought through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private insurer. The program typically has a 30-day waiting period before coverage starts, so buying after a forecast is too late.

Douglas County places its northern and eastern areas in the Sweetwater Creek floodplain and the southeast in the Chattahoochee floodplain. The county flood brochure and floodplain maps help you see where your lot falls.

Questions to Ask Your Carrier

  • Does my policy cover water damage from a sudden pipe failure, and what is the deductible?
  • Is sewer or drain backup included or optional?
  • How does the policy treat mold that follows a covered water loss?
  • Does coverage require emergency mitigation, and how should I document it?

Keep a written record of the answers, including the date and the name of the person who gave them. A short call before a loss is much easier than a long one after.

Reading the Declarations Page

The declarations page lists your deductible, your coverage limits and any endorsements. Look for the water-related lines, such as water backup, service line coverage and ordinance or law coverage. Each one fills a gap that a base policy leaves open.

Deductibles often differ by peril. A wind or hail deductible can be a percentage of the home value, while a water deductible may be a flat dollar amount. Knowing which one applies helps you decide whether a small loss is worth reporting.

Mold After a Covered Loss

Many policies limit mold coverage or tie it to a covered water event. If a covered pipe break is dried promptly, mold is far less likely, so fast mitigation protects both the home and the claim. If drying is delayed, the insurer may argue the mold came from neglect instead of the original loss.

Keep drying logs and photos with dates. They show that you acted quickly and give the adjuster a clean timeline.
